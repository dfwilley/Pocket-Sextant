Calibration

1. Camera scale

Teaches the app how many screen pixels equal one degree, so it can correct for the sun not being dead-center. Do this once, outdoors, in sunlight.

2. Index correction (IC)

Every phone's tilt sensor is off by a small fixed amount, like a sextant's index error. Shoot one to five phone sights at a spot whose position you know, such as home. The app compares them with the true sun altitude and works out the correction.

Settings

Used for the refraction correction (light bending in the air). It matters most for low sun.

How it works

A marine sextant measures the sun's height above the sea horizon. Pocket Sextant measures it against gravity instead, using the phone's tilt sensor. That's the same idea as the bubble sextants that aircraft navigators used. Like those, it averages many readings to smooth out the shakes.

Each sight gives a line of position (LOP): a line you're somewhere on. Two or more lines taken at different times, with the sun at different bearings, cross at your position. That crossing is the fix.

Quick start

  1. Calibrate once (Setup tab): camera scale, then index correction at home.
  2. Shoot: tap Start Sextant, hold the phone upright, put the sun in the ring, and tap MARK. Hold steady until the bar fills.
  3. Wait 2 to 3 hours and shoot again, so the sun has moved around the sky. Three sights (morning, near noon, afternoon) give the best fix.
  4. Fix tab: enter the ship's course and speed if you were moving, then tap Compute Fix.
  5. Tap Check against GPS to see how you did.

What accuracy to expect

One arcminute (1/60 of a degree) of altitude error moves your line by one nautical mile. A good marine sextant is good to about 1 arcminute. A phone's tilt sensor, after calibration, is more like 5 to 30 arcminutes. So expect a phone fix within roughly 5 to 50 nautical miles. That's good enough to tell which island you're near, and about what an aircraft bubble sextant did. These figures are estimates; your phone may do better or worse, and the GPS check will tell you.

Time matters too: a clock that's 4 seconds off moves your longitude by 1 arcminute. The phone's clock is normally accurate.

Using a real sextant

On the Log tab, tap + Add sextant sight and enter your reading, the UTC time, height of eye, limb and index correction. The app does the full sight reduction (no tables needed) and plots the fix.

Words used here

Hs
Sextant altitude: the raw reading.
Ho
Observed altitude: the reading after corrections for index error, refraction, parallax, dip and limb.
Hc
Computed altitude: what the sun's height would be at a trial position.
Intercept
Ho minus Hc, in nautical miles. Positive means "toward" the sun's bearing, negative means "away."
Zn
The sun's true bearing.
Dip
Correction for your eye being above the sea, which lowers the visible horizon. It doesn't apply to phone sights, which use gravity, not the horizon.
Running fix
A fix from sights taken at different times, with the earlier lines moved along the ship's course and speed.

For fun and practice — not for actual navigation. Sun positions come from standard astronomical formulas, accurate to better than 1 arcminute.
